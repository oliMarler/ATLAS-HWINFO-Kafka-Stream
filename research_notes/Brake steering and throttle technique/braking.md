# Braking technique in endurance race cars (GTP/Hypercar without ABS, GT3 with ABS, LMP2) and how coaches judge it from telemetry

Labels: **[PRO]** = real-world professional source (driver, team, supplier, pro data/coaching school). **[SIM]** = sim-racing source (iRacing official, or a sim coaching site; Le Mans Ultimate (LMU) is named where the source is LMU, not iRacing). **[single source]** = only one source found for the claim. **[search summary]** = the page could not be fetched (403/402) or was not fetched, so the claim comes from the search engine's summary of it and should be treated as lower confidence. Anything in an "Inferences" list is my reasoning, not a sourced fact.

## 1. Initial application: how fast to peak, how high a peak, why it must come early, threshold braking without ABS, and how lockups show in data

### Takeaway
Every source agrees on the shape: a fast, firm, smooth rise to peak pressure right at the start of the zone, then a steady bleed-off. In a high-downforce car the most grip is available at the top of the zone, so a soft or late build wastes it. The only time-to-peak numbers found are anecdotal: about 0.45-0.47 s for a pro in an IMSA LMP-C car, against a 0.2 s engineering yardstick and a "<0.4 s" target for amateurs. The only peak-pressure percentage for iRacing (85-90%) comes from one vendor article with a known factual error. Without ABS, lockups show most clearly in wheel-speed data. Brake pressure alone is a weak lockup indicator.

### Cited Findings
- [PRO] HP Academy's professional data-analysis course: "the quicker we build brake pressure, the quicker we can get the tyres to peak slip ratio." The ideal build rate depends on the car, tyres, suspension and the stability margin at that point on track. — [HP Academy, Braking performance / braking technique](https://hpacademy.com/courses/professional-motorsport-data-analysis/braking-performance-braking-technique)
- [PRO] Same course: peak pressure "should always occur at the start of the stop", followed by a smooth release. It names a fault pattern: drivers "apply peak pressure, realise they brake too early and release", then reapply. It also says to minimise the throttle-to-brake gap, because coasting time is lost time. — [HP Academy](https://hpacademy.com/courses/professional-motorsport-data-analysis/braking-performance-braking-technique)
- [PRO] Same course: high-downforce cars need "a consistent drop in pedal pressure to maintain peak slip ratio and avoid locking a tyre" as speed (and so downforce-driven tyre load) falls. Low-downforce cars can hold a flatter, plateau-shaped trace. — [HP Academy](https://hpacademy.com/courses/professional-motorsport-data-analysis/braking-performance-braking-technique)
- [PRO, single source, search summary] On a Rennlist data-acquisition thread, a poster reports that the fastest IMSA LMP-C pole lap at Sebring and a lap near the VIR record both took about **0.45-0.47 s to reach maximum brake pressure** in most braking zones, and notes these cars have far more aero than any GT car. The same thread says club/HPDE drivers often take "well over 0.33 s", some 0.8-1.0 s. It cites an **OptimumG paper giving ~0.2 s as a rough yardstick**, and the poster suggests **under 0.4 s** as a reasonable target for a sprung, damped race car. The thread is about 8 years old and anecdotal, and the page itself returned 403. — [Rennlist: Understanding brake pressure (psi)](https://rennlist.com/forums/data-acquisition-and-analysis-for-racing-and-de/1120029-understanding-brake-pressure-psi-what-is-it-telling-me.html)
- [SIM] Coach Dave Academy brake-trace guide: top drivers apply the brakes "firmly and quickly" to peak, which shows as a sharp rise in the initial slope. A gradual application can mean low confidence in the braking zone or the reference markers. The stated aim is "a smooth but rapid initial application". The initial phase shows whether the driver is threshold braking; if not, work on technique or check that the pedal setup gives full pressure easily. — [Coach Dave Academy, Delta guide: understanding brake traces](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/a-delta-guide-understanding-brake-traces-to-be-faster)
- [PRO] Driver61: a tyre generally gives most grip at "3-10% slippage" (e.g. car at 100 mph, tyre surface at 90 mph = 10%). Threshold braking means staying at the edge of grip, just before the tyre breaks traction. Slight under-rotation of the tyre is the warning sign of an imminent lock. Build pressure toward maximum "without shocking the car". A locked front tyre removes steering, and the usual fix is to release slightly. Braking 10 m later saved about 0.2 s in its example [single source; one context]. — [Driver61, Ultimate guide to braking](https://driver61.com/uni/braking/)
- [PRO] Bryan Van Noy (NASA Speed News): threshold braking means maximum pressure quickly, without locking the tyres or triggering ABS. — [NASA Speed News, The art of releasing the brakes](https://nasaspeed.news/columns/driver-instruction/the-art-of-releasing-the-brakes/)
- [SIM] Coach Dave, iRacing Porsche 963 GTP guide: the 963 has no ABS, so "your foot on the brake pedal is solely responsible for getting the car stopped before the corner". The car "has a tendency to lock up its front tyres". Entry understeer puts more load on the fronts and makes lockups more likely. "Being lighter on the pedal when the effect of downforce is less prevalent will make braking easier." The guide recommends braking in a straight line before turn-in. — [Coach Dave Academy, iRacing guide: Porsche 963 GTP](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/iracing-guide-porsche-963-gtp/)
- [SIM, LMU not iRacing] Traxion: "as speeds reduce under braking, so does aerodynamic grip, making wheel lock-ups much more likely." — [Traxion, LMU: how to master Hypercar braking](https://traxion.gg/le-mans-ultimate-how-to-master-hypercar-braking/)
- [SIM, LMU not iRacing, search summary] Coach Dave's LMU Valkyrie guide: the Valkyrie is "more akin to an LMDh car, where you have to brake earlier". Lock-ups are common, so brake "just at the edge of locking". As a safe baseline, set brake force to maximum and disable brake migration, because the car tends to lock front and rear equally. — [Coach Dave Academy, LMU Aston Martin Valkyrie guide](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/lmu-aston-martin-valkyrie-guide-the-inside-line/)
- [SIM, single vendor source, search summary] Fanatec: for iRacing closed-wheel cars (GT or LMDh), reach a **peak around 85-90%** of the braking capacity of the in-game settings and pedal combination. It recommends a slightly gentler ramp than in formula cars, held a little longer, and says optimal braking force with GT cars typically ranges **70-90%** depending on approach speed, weather and track. Caution: the same article advises "avoid engaging the ABS" in iRacing GT *and LMDh* cars, but iRacing GTP/LMDh cars have no ABS (contradicted by Coach Dave's 963 guide above). So the article is unreliable on prototype specifics. — [Fanatec, Sim racing braking technique](https://fanatec.com/eu/en/explorer/games/gaming-tips/sim-racing-braking-technique/); contradicted by [Coach Dave 963 guide](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/iracing-guide-porsche-963-gtp/)
- [PRO, search summary] Lockup detection: lockups show as sharp downward spikes in a wheel-speed trace during braking. Telling a front lock from a rear lock needs separate front and rear wheel-speed traces. Brake pressure sensors alone are a weak lockup indicator ("you really want wheel speed input for actual traction conditions"). — [HP Academy, Essential sensors: speed](https://www.hpacademy.com/courses/data-analysis-fundamentals/essential-sensors-speed/); [HP Academy forum: brake pressure sensors](https://www.hpacademy.com/es/foro/discusion-general-sobre-afinacion/show/brake-pressure-sensors/)
- [PRO/kart, search summary] AiM's brake-sensor guide: spikes in the pressure trace suggest stabbing the pedal, and a mid-zone pressure drop suggests releasing too early. — [AiM blog, brake pressure sensors](https://www.aimshop.com/blogs/news/save-a-second-off-your-lap-time-for-under-200)

### Inferences
- Why peak must be early (physics reasoning, consistent with HP Academy, Traxion and Coach Dave above): aero load scales roughly with speed squared, so the highest braking force the tyres can take comes at the very start of the zone. Pressure built slowly over the first few tenths spends the highest-grip part of the zone below the limit, and that time cannot be recovered later because the limit only falls.
- The time-to-peak figures conflict: pros at ~0.45 s in an LMP-C versus a 0.2 s yardstick. Possible explanations, none sourced: "maximum pressure" may be defined differently (first plateau vs absolute max); pros may deliberately shape the last part of the rise to let the platform settle; or the 0.2 s figure is a vehicle-dynamics ideal. For coaching, a time-to-peak *relative to the driver's own best lap and to a reference lap* is safer than an absolute threshold.
- Peak pressure as a percentage is setup- and rig-dependent in iRacing (brake pressure/master-cylinder settings, pedal calibration). The 85-90% figure should not be used as a universal target. The better test is whether the driver gets near the lockup limit at the top of the zone, judged from longitudinal g.
- Lockup signature with only brake %, speed, long-g, steering and lap distance (no wheel speed), inferred from the physics: (a) longitudinal deceleration *drops* while brake pressure is flat or rising, because a locked tyre has less grip than one at peak slip; (b) speed decay flattens compared with the reference lap at the same distance; (c) a front lock is often followed by a pressure dip (the driver releases to recover) and small steering corrections, or by a missed apex and late turn-in; (d) a rear lock is more likely to show a sudden steering correction (countersteer) under braking. These need validating against iRacing data. If wheel-speed channels can be read from iRacing telemetry, they should take priority (not verified here).
- Too-soft or too-slow initial hit: the cost shows as lower peak long-g than the reference in the first 20-30% of the zone, and usually a later, longer zone or an earlier brake point to compensate (inferred from HP Academy and Coach Dave).

### Gaps
- No published, current-era (2023+) time-to-peak or peak-pressure figures for GTP/LMDh, LMH, LMP2 or GT3 race cars. The only numbers are an anecdotal LMP-C forum post and an OptimumG yardstick known second-hand.
- No source gave a typical peak brake-pressure percentage specifically for iRacing GTP cars (Porsche 963, Valkyrie). The Fanatec figure is generic and flawed.
- No source quantified how much lap time a slow initial hit costs per tenth of extra build time.
- Could not verify whether iRacing exposes per-wheel speed in live telemetry for lockup detection.

## 2. Modulation and release: tapering pressure as speed and downforce fall, trail braking into the corner, abrupt-release effects, and how release is measured and coached

### Takeaway
After the early peak, pressure should fall steadily, because in an aero car grip falls with speed. Once steering starts, pressure should come down roughly in proportion to steering, tapering toward the apex rather than snapping off. An abrupt release unloads the front and usually produces entry understeer rather than rotation, and in aero cars it also costs downforce. Pro coaches treat release as the main skill: Ross Bentley says he coaches it more than anything else. The standard data tool is an XY plot of brake pressure against steering angle, plus an overlay of the release segment against a reference lap.

### Cited Findings
- [PRO] HP Academy: during trail braking "we need to reduce the brake pressure in proportion to the steering input". The target is pressure "steadily and linearly reduced as the car approaches the apex". **Diagnostic:** plot steering angle against brake pressure on an XY chart. Points above the linear target line mean too much pressure is held while steering; points below mean too little. Smaller between-corner applications should be gentler and more symmetrical so the car stays settled under lateral load. Before comparing braking points or technique between laps, check that the overlaid channels are aligned. — [HP Academy](https://hpacademy.com/courses/professional-motorsport-data-analysis/braking-performance-braking-technique)
- [SIM] Coach Dave: "The brake pressure should gradually taper off as steering input increases." Trail braking shows as a taper at the end of the zone rather than a full lift, keeping the fronts loaded for turn-in. A sudden drop near the end of the zone, compared with the reference, means releasing too quickly. That can destabilise the car, and in a car with significant aero, downforce drops too. Releasing too soon can reflect low confidence in entry speed. Staying at maximum pressure longer than the reference means "you've been on the brakes too long". — [Coach Dave Academy, brake traces](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/a-delta-guide-understanding-brake-traces-to-be-faster)
- [PRO] Van Noy: "the true difference-maker for faster corner entries lies not in how hard, or how late, you hit the brakes" but in the release. **By corner type:** slow corners and hairpins: hold pressure deeper and ease off approaching the apex. Medium corners: a quicker but still progressive release, then straight to throttle without coasting so "the rear end stays planted". High-speed corners: softer, longer initial pressure, then a smooth, gradual release, because "Too much brake pressure tends to upset most cars in the highest-speed corners." Trailing light pressure keeps front load up, which reduces understeer, and lightens the rear so the car rotates more freely. "The steering wheel is also a brake." In back-to-back slow corners, a quick dab while turning can help rotation. — [NASA Speed News](https://nasaspeed.news/columns/driver-instruction/the-art-of-releasing-the-brakes/)
- [PRO, search summary] Speed Secrets (Ross Bentley's coaching site): the rate of release manages corner-entry speed and front load. Too quick a release unloads the front and can cause understeer. Too slow a release over-slows the car and can overload the front tyres. An abrupt release is more likely to cause understeer than rotation. Many drivers use the same release timing and rate in every corner, "but that can't be right": some corners reward an early, fast release, others a late, slow one. — [Speed Secrets](https://speedsecrets.com/?p=26035) (exact page for each line not confirmed; search returned several speedsecrets.com pages)
- [PRO, search summary] Speed Secrets Q&A on over-braking understeer: if releasing early causes understeer, the driver may be unloading the front too soon. The fix is to move the whole braking zone later so the front stays loaded longer. — [Speed Secrets: How do I stop over-braking and causing understeer](https://speedsecrets.com/q-how-do-i-stop-over-braking-and-causing-my-car-to-understeer/)
- [PRO, search summary] Ross Bentley says he spends more time coaching drivers on brake release than on anything else, and suggests experimenting with release timing and rate. — [Speed Secrets](https://speedsecrets.com/?p=39183) (page attribution from search summary, not fetched)
- [PRO] Driver61: trail braking is continued braking "at a much-reduced rate" while steering in, to adjust balance rather than to slow the car. Ease off smoothly approaching turn-in so the nose rises to a balanced platform. For faster corners, brush the brakes lightly over a longer distance to limit weight transfer. — [Driver61](https://driver61.com/uni/braking/)
- [SIM, search summary] SimXPro: most trail-braking mistakes are timing errors: releasing too fast or turning too late. Its fixes: start with a "long release", separate the brake-release timing from the steering timing, and reduce steering rate. — [SimXPro, Trail braking mistakes](https://simxpro.com/blogs/guides/trail-braking-mistakes-9-common-errors-and-the-simple-fix-for-each)
- [SIM, search summary] Track Titan study plan: controlling the speed of release changes the car's angle and weight balance into the corner. — [Track Titan, Braking: Taming the trail](https://www.tracktitan.io/study-plans/da7fb274-38f7-11ed-a261-0242ac120002-braking-taming-the-trail)
- [SIM, LMU] Traxion: with brake migration, bias moves rearward as pedal travel falls, "aiding trail braking". — [Traxion](https://traxion.gg/le-mans-ultimate-how-to-master-hypercar-braking/)

### Inferences
- Release metrics that fit the available channels (my construction, built from the HP Academy and Coach Dave principles): (1) **release start** = lap distance where pressure first falls below ~90% of that zone's peak; (2) **release rate** = slope of pressure (%/s or %/m) from the end of the peak to zero, compared with the reference lap; (3) **brake at turn-in** = pressure at the first meaningful steering input; (4) **trail length** = distance from turn-in to brake = 0; (5) **steering-vs-brake linearity** = residual from the HP Academy XY line; (6) **snap-off** = maximum negative pressure gradient in the last ~10% of the zone; (7) **coast gap** = time with brake ≈ 0 and throttle ≈ 0 before throttle pickup.
- Entry-balance diagnosis from data: entry understeer that follows a large snap-off (fast pressure drop just before or at turn-in) points to the release. Entry oversteer with lots of pressure held into high steering angles points to over-trailing or rearward bias/migration (see section 3). Both follow from the Speed Secrets, Van Noy and Coach Dave principles, but the mapping is my inference.
- In a high-downforce GTP car, the high-speed corner advice (softer, longer, smoother) matters most, because an abrupt release there moves the aero platform as well as the load (inferred from Coach Dave's aero remark and Van Noy).

### Gaps
- No source gives numeric target release rates (%/s), typical brake % at turn-in, or typical trail lengths for prototypes or GT3.
- No pro source specific to GTP/LMDh trail-braking technique (driver interviews on how much they trail) was found.

## 3. Brake bias and migration through a lap and a stint (forward vs rearward, fuel and tyre effects, GTP/LMDh migration, iRacing's implementation, lockup diagnosis and fixes)

### Takeaway
Conventional guidance: forward bias = stability and more entry understeer; rearward = more rotation but rear-lock risk. Bias is trimmed in small steps as fuel burns and tyres wear, and you move bias away from whichever axle locks first. Migration makes bias pressure-dependent. iRacing GTP officially defines bias at 100% pedal as the base setting and bias at 0% pedal as base plus the migration gain. One LMU source uses the opposite reference point, so a coach must know which convention applies. Real Porsche 963 drivers use cockpit-adjustable migration shaping (separate longitudinal and lateral terms) and make several bias/migration changes per lap. Regen blending at the rear axle also shifts effective balance with battery state of charge.

### Cited Findings
- [SIM, official iRacing] 2025 Season 3 Patch 3 release notes, GTP class: "Fixed an issue where brake migration was being applied at half of the stated value." "The ramping reference location has also been adjusted." "At 100% pedal input, the brake bias is equal to the basic brake bias setting." "At 0% pedal input, the brake bias is equal to the basic brake bias setting plus the brake migration gain." To adjust existing setups, the recommendation is to "halve the current brake migration setting" "and increase brake bias forwards by 1-1.25%." The same patch added a brake migration adjustment to the Acura ARX-06 "to bring it into line with the other GTP class cars". For the Ferrari 499P, migration "was already working correctly" and no physics changes were applied. — [iRacing 2025 S3 Patch 3 release notes](https://support.iracing.com/support/solutions/articles/31000176763-2025-season-3-patch-3-release-notes-2025-06-27-02-)
- [PRO] Porsche 963 brake system (Chris Perkins, Motorsport.com, republished Jun 13 2025): LMDh front brakes are hydraulic. The rear uses Bosch's Electronic Braking System (brake-by-wire) to blend friction, MGU regen and engine braking, with no fixed split. Bosch's Jordan Krell describes migration as dynamic bias on the pressure side that can shift bias rearward as the car slows into a corner. Felipe Nasr says the migration shaping is cockpit-adjustable, with plus/minus settings for longitudinal (straight-line) and lateral (with steering) braking. Drivers make "a handful" of bias and migration adjustments per lap, tailored to each corner. The MGU (limit ~180 kW) can supply up to 40% of total rear braking in the hardest zones (e.g. Indianapolis T1). Regen depends on state of charge, which changes constantly through a lap and stint. Nasr: "The longer brakes are always the trickiest", because more systems are combining inputs, and the aim is to "try not to lock any of the tires". On hydraulic brakes drivers feel the rear lock and release, and brake-by-wire can interfere with that feedback. — [Motorsport.com via Yahoo Sports, Point 'Brake': Porsche 963 braking system](https://sports.yahoo.com/article/point-brake-complexities-behind-porsches-120003063.html)
- [PRO, conflicting] The fetched summary of that same article says "more front bias gives a looser car with more rotation on entry, while more rear bias stabilizes the car and adds understeer". That is the reverse of every other source here, so it is probably an error in the article or in the summary. **Do not use it.** — [Yahoo Sports/Motorsport.com](https://sports.yahoo.com/article/point-brake-complexities-behind-porsches-120003063.html); contradicted by [Speed Secrets bias Q&A](https://speedsecrets.com/q-should-i-adjust-brake-bias-during-a-race-how-do-i-know-when-to-adjust-brake-bias/) and [BoxThisLap GTP guide](https://boxthislap.org/iracing-gtp-endurance-guide-beginners/)
- [SIM, LMU not iRacing] Traxion: "Brake migration is like active brake balance, moving the balance forwards when the brake pedal travel reaches 100%." Example: an "initial brake bias setting of 54:46" with migration 2.0F gives an "actual brake bias of 56:44 when the brake pedal hits 100% travel". In LMU, migration adjusts 0-2.5 in 0.5 steps and can be changed on the fly. The article calls 2.5F "the optimal brake migration setting for all Hypercars" [single source]. LMH cars are "susceptible to losing front braking performance during longer stints". Fade moves the bias rearward, making the rear easier to lock. With a full battery (no regen available), an LMH "is more likely to lock its rear wheels" and an LMDh "is more likely to lock its fronts", so keep the battery below 100% and drain it before the start. — [Traxion](https://traxion.gg/le-mans-ultimate-how-to-master-hypercar-braking/)
- [SIM, LMU, conflicting] Coach Dave's LMU Valkyrie guide recommends *disabling* migration as a safe baseline, which conflicts with Traxion's "2.5F optimal for all Hypercars". — [Coach Dave LMU Valkyrie](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/lmu-aston-martin-valkyrie-guide-the-inside-line/) vs [Traxion](https://traxion.gg/le-mans-ultimate-how-to-master-hypercar-braking/)
- [SIM] BoxThisLap iRacing GTP guide: moving bias forward usually gives more stability, and moving it progressively rearward uses more of the rear axle's braking capacity. — [BoxThisLap, iRacing GTP endurance guide](https://boxthislap.org/iracing-gtp-endurance-guide-beginners/)
- [SIM] Coach Dave (iRacing 963): "Moving the brake bias backwards can help with reducing front locking." Bias is offered as a quick, safer fix for understeer than changing the front anti-roll bar. — [Coach Dave 963](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/iracing-guide-porsche-963-gtp/)
- [PRO] Speed Secrets "Ask Ross" bias Q&A (attribution to Ross Bentley inferred from the site): **front lockup** symptoms are "the steering feels numb" under hard braking, or the car "suddenly pulls to one side or the other under braking", so move bias rearward. **Rear instability:** "the rear of the car feels like it's wiggling" and the car oversteers on entry, so move bias forward. Balance changes "due to fuel load burning off" and with tyre wear. Make small changes, because you can "get yourself mixed up easily if you make too big a change", and periodically return to baseline to check. "Ideally, you want all four tires to lock up at the exact same time if you brake too hard." — [Speed Secrets, Should I adjust brake bias during a race?](https://speedsecrets.com/q-should-i-adjust-brake-bias-during-a-race-how-do-i-know-when-to-adjust-brake-bias/)
- [PRO, search summary] Speed Secrets bias test: find which end locks first by deliberately braking too hard, then move bias away from that end in small steps (about half a percent). — [Speed Secrets, How can I tell whether my brake bias is set correctly](https://speedsecrets.com/q-how-can-i-tell-whether-i-have-my-brake-bias-set-correctly/)
- [SIM, LMU guides, search summary] One guide finds a baseline by moving bias rearward until the rear becomes unstable on entry, then going 1-2 clicks forward. If the fronts wear faster, move bias gradually rearward as the stint goes on. Swinging bias more than 3-4% from baseline usually creates more problems than it solves. Early in a stint, while tyres warm, move bias slightly forward to limit braking oversteer, then back once temperatures come in. (The search summary did not say which of these two pages each line came from.) — [Simgasm, LMU: how to avoid lock-ups in Hypercar](https://simgasm.com/blogs/news/le-mans-ultimate-how-to-avoid-lock-ups-in-hypercar); [SimRacingCockpit, LMU: how to drive LMGT3 and Hypercar](https://simracingcockpit.gg/le-mans-ultimate-how-to-drive-the-lmgt3-and-hypercar/)
- [General reference] Brake migration: brake balance that changes dynamically with the force on the brake pedal. — [Wikipedia, Brake balance](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Brake_bias)

### Inferences
- **Convention difference (important for a coaching assistant):** iRacing GTP fixes the base bias at 100% pedal and applies the migration gain as pedal falls toward 0%. Traxion's LMU description fixes base bias at low pedal and adds forward shift at 100% travel. The same migration number therefore means different absolute bias at peak pressure in the two sims. The coach should report iRacing's displayed base bias as the *peak-pressure* bias, with migration acting on the trail/release phase.
- The patch note does not state the sign of the migration gain (whether it moves bias rearward or forward at low pedal). Real-world LMDh practice (Krell: rearward as the car slows) and the LMU description both point to *rearward as pressure falls*, which helps rotation on trail. That direction for iRacing is inferred and should be confirmed in-garage via the setup tooltip.
- Practical consequences, inferred from the sourced bias principles: (a) more migration = more rotation and rear-lock risk in the trail phase, with no change at peak pressure; (b) a driver who trails deeper (higher pressure into the corner) gets less migration effect than one who releases early; (c) a rear lock late in the zone at low pressure suggests too much migration, while a front lock at the top of the zone suggests base bias too far forward.
- Through a stint, as fuel burns the car gets lighter (and in most prototypes the rear may unload relatively, since the tank is near the centre). Front tyre wear also reduces front braking capability. Both are cited reasons to trim bias (Speed Secrets, LMU guides), but the *direction* depends on the car and is not sourced for GTP specifically.
- Hybrid effect: rear regen share varies with state of charge (Porsche/Bosch), so rear-axle braking and lock tendency can vary lap to lap. In iRacing GTP, if regen is modelled, a full battery may change rear-lock behaviour (Traxion says this for LMU). Whether iRacing models this for each car is not verified.

### Gaps
- No access to the iRacing Porsche 963 or Valkyrie user manuals' brake bias/migration/brake-pressure sections. iRacing's sign convention, the migration range and the "ramping reference location" are not documented beyond the patch note.
- Not verified whether the iRacing Valkyrie (LMH) has hybrid regen affecting braking. The real Valkyrie LMH's powertrain was not confirmed in this research.
- No sourced GTP-specific bias numbers (typical % front) or migration settings for iRacing.
- No pro source quantifying bias changes per hour of an endurance stint.

## 4. Endurance considerations: brake temperature/wear over long stints, consistency, traffic, cold tyres after a stop, and braking style vs front tyre wear and flat-spotting

### Takeaway
Hypercar carbon brakes have a temperature window. Brembo gives a 200 °C minimum for optimum performance, ~400 °C race average and >1,000 °C peaks, and separately a 250-850 °C operating range. Drivers are expected to manage disc/pad temperature and modulate pressure to avoid premature wear, while teams trade duct cooling against drag and can use regen to unload the friction brakes. Out-laps on cold tyres and brakes call for caution and possibly a forward bias trim. Lockups create flat spots that can fail a tyre later in the stint. Found no solid sources on braking in traffic or on brake-point consistency metrics.

### Cited Findings
- [PRO] Brembo, via the Le Mans 24 Hours site: Hypercar discs are carbon and need "a temperature of at least 200°C for optimum performance". Disc temperature averages "around 400°C" in the race and "can exceed 1000°C in heavy braking situations". Brake ducts add drag that can cost top speed, so cooling is traded against aero. Drivers must control disc and pad temperatures and modulate pressure to avoid premature wear. Hybrid energy recovery varies with battery charge and cell temperature, and the brake-by-wire friction system supplements the motors because they cannot supply all the braking force. — [24h-lemans.com, Brembo sheds light on Hypercar brake systems](https://www.24h-lemans.com/en/news/brembo-sheds-light-on-24-hours-of-le-mans-hypercar-brake-systems-59607)
- [PRO, search summary] Brembo's Hypercar carbon discs are quoted as working from about **250 °C to 850 °C**. ### Inferences
- For a no-ABS GTP car over a stint, the costs of style rank roughly (my synthesis): (1) lockups are the most expensive, because a flat spot persists for the rest of the stint (vibration, and failure risk per the Pérez case) and a locked front loses the corner; (2) heavy trailing deep into high steering angles loads the front tyres in combined slip and likely raises front wear; (3) over-long zones at high pressure add brake heat. That fits Brembo's call to modulate pressure to avoid premature wear, but no source quantifies any of it.
- Cold-tyre/out-lap coaching: expect lower peak long-g and earlier lockups on the first lap after a stop. The coach should judge the out-lap against a "cold" reference, or flag lockups without penalising slightly earlier brake points.
- Brake-point consistency over a stint: there is no sourced metric. A practical measure would be the standard deviation of brake-onset lap distance per corner across green-flag laps, excluding traffic-affected laps. Driver61's "10 m later ≈ 0.2 s" (single example) gives a rough scale for why metres matter.
- In GT3, sitting deep in ABS for a whole stint is a tyre/consistency question rather than a raw-pace one. Sources disagree on whether it costs pace (Speed Secrets: fine in a Porsche GT3; iRacing forum: trimming 5-10% may gain a tenth).

### Gaps

- No current LMDh/LMH-specific pad or disc wear figures per stint, or driver-side brake-saving technique (e.g. lift-and-coast effect on brake temperature) beyond Brembo's general statement.
- No source on how iRacing models brake temperature, fade or wear for GTP cars, or flat-spot persistence.

## 5. Numbers and telemetry markers: peak pressures, time-to-peak, consistency, deceleration (g) GTP/LMP vs GT3, what engineers look at, and example coaching phrases

### Takeaway
Hard numbers are scarce and mostly old or anecdotal. Prototype time-to-peak is ~0.45 s (one LMP-C forum report), the yardstick ~0.2 s, and the amateur target <0.4 s. iRacing GT/LMDh peak is ~85-90% (one flawed vendor source). Prototype peak deceleration is ~3.5 g (LMP1 at Le Mans, search summary only). A road-going 911 GT3 manages 1.4 g; no race-spec GT3 figure was found. Pro data analysis reads the trace in phases: coast gap, build rate, peak timing and duration, bleed-off shape, trail-vs-steering linearity, and snap-off at release. Each phase is always compared with a reference lap.

### Cited Findings
- [PRO, single source, search summary] Time to max pressure: ~0.45-0.47 s (pro, IMSA LMP-C, Sebring/VIR); OptimumG yardstick ~0.2 s; poster's target <0.4 s; amateurs often >0.33 s, some 0.8-1.0 s. — [Rennlist thread](https://rennlist.com/forums/data-acquisition-and-analysis-for-racing-and-de/1120029-understanding-brake-pressure-psi-what-is-it-telling-me.html)

### Inferences
- Derived figure: 200 mph to 60 mph (≈89.4 to 26.8 m/s) in 3.2 s is an *average* of ≈19.6 m/s², about 2.0 g (my calculation from the Autocar figure). That fits a ~3.5 g peak at high speed falling as downforce drops, so a prototype long-g trace should be strongly front-loaded, peaking early and declining through the zone. A GT3 trace should be flatter, around the 1.4 g region or above, since race GT3s have slicks and aero (the race-car number is unsourced).
- Suggested telemetry markers for the iRacing coach (my construction, each tied to a sourced principle above): time-to-peak (s, brake onset to 95% of zone peak); peak brake % and peak long-g; distance from brake onset to peak long-g; peak duration; bleed-off slope; brake % at turn-in; release rate and snap-off; trail-vs-steering residual; coast gap; brake-onset distance spread across laps; lockup flags (long-g drop under steady or rising pressure, plus corrections).
- Example coaching phrases built from the sourced principles (my wording, *not* quotes from any source; use as templates): "Get to the top of the pedal sooner; you're taking [x] s to peak against [y] s on your best lap." "You're peaking late; the car can take the most brake at the highest speed." "Bleed it off as the speed drops; the downforce goes with it." "Don't snap off the brake at turn-in; ease it out as you add steering." "You're carrying too much brake into the apex; release earlier, or move the zone later." "Front lock at the top of the zone: try bias a click rearward." "Rear moving late in the zone: less migration or bias forward." "Out-lap: brakes and tyres are cold, so brake a bit earlier and lighter until they come in."
